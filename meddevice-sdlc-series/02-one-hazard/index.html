Transcript

Every example uses one made-up device: a motorized rehab device in which a PC app sends settings to controller firmware that drives a motor moving a patient's arm or leg. Every number, ID and version in the story is made up.

Why 300 ms?. In Episode 1, a made-up team wrote a requirement: if the app stops, the motor stops within 300 milliseconds. But why 300 milliseconds? What could hurt the patient, and how badly? This is Episode 2: one hazard, from risk to test.

The big picture. Here's the one-page picture from Episode 1. This time the risk band lifts out: ISO 14971. FDA inspectors look at risk analysis too: their citations for missing or inadequate risk analysis rose from 26 to 42 to 47 over three fiscal years.

The freeze, and the risk thread. Back on the made-up rehab device, suppose the app freezes mid-motion. Stop on screen does nothing, and the motor keeps moving the limb. This episode follows that one risk, like a thread, from hazard to test.

Hazard to harm. The hazard is the motor's energy, a potential source of harm, and the freeze is one way it gets out of control. The hazardous situation: the limb keeps moving past its set range. The harm: a strain or sprain.

Estimating the risk. Next, the team estimates the risk: how likely the harm is, and how severe. In my experience, teams often assume a software failure will happen. The made-up team rates the sprain a non-serious injury.

Risk control, in order. Risk controls come in a set order. First, safe design, the top choice: here, a made-up mechanical stop, so the joint can't move past a safe outer range. Second, protective measures in the device: the firmware stops the motor if the app's "still alive" heartbeat goes quiet. Last, information for safety, like labeling, warnings and training, as the last resort.

The software safety class. Now the software safety class, A, B or C. To set it, IEC 62304 also assumes the software fails, and counts only controls outside it. So a check inside the app wouldn't lower its class, but the firmware stop can. To me, where a control lives matters as much as what it does.

Risk control → requirements. Next, the control becomes requirements, with made-up numbers. The system requirement: if the app goes silent, motion stops within 300 milliseconds. The software requirements: a heartbeat every 50 milliseconds, and a firmware stop if it's missing for 150. So why 300? At a top speed of 30 degrees a second, that's at most 9 degrees past the set range, a made-up limit agreed with clinicians.

Verification. Then the team verifies the stop. The made-up test tries four ways the app can stop: closed, frozen, cable pulled, PC asleep. The motor must stop every time.

A new risk from the control. But a control can bring a new risk, which ISO 14971 asks teams to look for: an instant stop could jerk the limb. So the team adds FW-05, a made-up firmware requirement: slow the motor smoothly to zero within 100 milliseconds. That's 150 milliseconds to notice, plus 100 to slow down: 250, still inside the 300.

Residual risk. Even with every control in place, some risk is left. The team checks that residual risk against the acceptance criteria in the risk management plan. If it's still too high, they add controls, or, when no more are practicable, weigh it against the medical benefit, and named people sign the decision.

Traceability. Traceability ties it all together: hazard, control, requirement, test. It shows each link is written down, though it can't prove every hazard was found.

Built right?. So the 300 milliseconds now has a reason, a control and a test. Now it has to be built right, and proved.

End card. Medical Device Software Lifecycle in Practice. Next: build it right.

Transcript

Every example uses one made-up device: a motorized rehab device in which a PC app sends settings to controller firmware that drives a motor moving a patient's arm or leg. Every number, ID and version in the story is made up.

The chaos. If you've ever built software for a medical device, you know the feeling. FDA, MDR, CDSCO, ISO 13485, IEC 62304, CAPA, SOUP, NCR. It can feel like chaos.

Why it's hard. There are good reasons for it. If the software fails, a patient can get hurt. Each market has its own regulator, and you have to prove what you did, in writing.

Who writes the rules. I've spent more than ten years building medical device software. This is how I make sense of it. ISO and IEC are international bodies that write standards. On their own, standards are voluntary; they become hard to avoid once a regulator recognizes them. Think of four layers: the law, the standards, the regulator's guidance, and your own procedures.

Three standards. For device software, three standards do most of the work. ISO 13485 is the quality management system, across the whole company. ISO 14971 is risk management: what could harm patients, users or others, and how it's controlled. And IEC 62304 is the software life cycle, from planning and requirements, through design and testing, to release and maintenance.

Where you sell. On top sit the rules and who checks them: FDA in the US, the MDR and notified bodies in Europe, and CDSCO in India. Different rules on top; mostly the same standards underneath.

The map. Put the questions in the order a device lives through them, and the tangle becomes a map. Each standard is a line, plus the regulators, plus the tools that help.

First stop: before you start. Is it a medical device? Intended use. Is the software the device itself, SaMD, or part of one? How risky is it? The device class. How does it reach the market? For example, a 510(k) or CE marking. How does the company work? Its quality system.

Next stop: design. What could hurt someone? It's traced from hazard to harm, then controlled, safe design first. The worst harm if the software fails, after outside safeguards, sets its safety class: A, B or C. The controls become requirements, and the design covers security and real-world use.

Next stop: build. Can a team work in sprints? Yes, when 'done' includes the evidence. What version is this? Configuration management. What came off the shelf? SOUP, like an open-source library, listed in the SBOM. Was it built right? Verification. Does everything link? Traceability.

Next stop: release. The software is released for use in the full device. Was the right thing built? Design validation. Is it safe enough to ship? Residual risk, weighed against benefit. Then design transfer to production. What goes to the regulator? The submission. Where's the proof? The design and development file.

Last stop: in the field. Every change to released software goes through change control. What didn't meet a requirement? An NCR. What if something goes wrong? Complaints, problem reports, and reporting on time. What stops it coming back? CAPA, when a problem is serious, repeats or runs deep. And who checks? Audits and inspections.

Tools and AI. In my experience, much of this can be automated. Tools like Jira and Confluence, test management, and a CI/CD pipeline can link requirements to tests on every build. AI drafts from those facts, and a named person reviews and signs. And the tools themselves are validated for their use.

Payoff. Once I knew the questions, there were fewer surprises, and the process started to help me.

What's coming. Over four short episodes, one made-up rehab device travels from the first map, through risk and evidence, to the day an auditor picks one bug. Medical Device Software Lifecycle in Practice. See you in Episode 1.

On screen, but not spoken

  • IEC 62304's steps, with their clauses:
    • Planning 5.1 · Requirements 5.2 · Architecture 5.3 · Design 5.4 · Coding 5.5 · Testing 5.6–5.7 · Release 5.8 · Maintenance 6
    • Alongside: risk management 7, configuration management 8, problem resolution 9
    • How deep each step goes depends on the software safety class (A, B, C).
  • Where IEC 62304 stands: FDA-recognized, and listed in CDSCO's software guidance. Under the EU MDR it's widely used, but as of October 2026 it isn't a harmonized standard.
  • Routes to market: 510(k), De Novo, PMA, CE marking, a license in India. In the US, some devices are exempt; in India, some class A devices only register.
  • SOUP (IEC 62304 3.29): software not developed for the device, like off-the-shelf software, or software without adequate development records.
  • Design validation sits outside IEC 62304 (clause 1.2). It's a quality-system step for the whole device (ISO 13485 7.3.7).
  • Tools: 21 CFR Part 11 covers electronic records and signatures; ISO 13485 4.1.6 asks for software used in the quality system to be validated for its use.

When something goes wrong

A report from the field: the motor took almost half a second to stop. Four paths after release, and the audit.

Transcript

Every example uses one made-up device: a motorized rehab device in which a PC app sends settings to controller firmware that drives a motor moving a patient's arm or leg. Every number, ID and version in the story is made up.

The hook. A therapist reports that the app stopped responding, and the motor took almost half a second to stop. In this made-up story, nobody was hurt, but the requirement says 300 milliseconds. What happens next? This is Episode 4: when something goes wrong, four paths, and the audit.

The big picture. Here's the one-page picture from Episode 1 again. Each flow on it ends in the same place: in the field, after release. That's where complaints, fixes, CAPA and audits come together, and it's the part this episode zooms into.

A complaint. The therapist's report is logged as a complaint. ISO 13485 expects each complaint to be recorded and evaluated, and if one isn't investigated, the reason is written down. Here, a safety stop missed its requirement, so the team investigates.

Is it reportable?. Then a question with a deadline: does the regulator need to know? If the problem could seriously hurt someone if it happened again, it usually has to be reported, within days or weeks, depending on the country and the event. The team decides, and writes down why.

Problem report and investigation. On the software side, IEC 62304 asks for a problem report. The team investigates the cause, checks what it means for safety, and updates the risk file. The logs show a clue: the slow stop came soon after the PC woke from sleep.

The fix, and the 510(k) question. The fix starts as a change request, approved before anyone changes the code. Then the team verifies the change, repeating any verification the change invalidated. The made-up device is cleared through a 510(k), so the team also asks: does this change need a new 510(k), or a documented decision? FDA's 2017 software-change guidance helps answer that, and either way, the reasoning is written down.

Four paths, side by side. So far, two paths have run: problem resolution and change control. Two more come from ISO 13485. A nonconformance report, or NCR, records product that missed a requirement. And CAPA, corrective and preventive action, goes after the cause. In my experience, it's for patterns, recurrence and serious problems, so not every bug is a CAPA. The four paths are separate, but linked.

CAPA: a pattern. Here's a pattern: three clinics report the same slow stop, always after the PC wakes from sleep, so the team opens a CAPA. The root cause goes deeper than the code: the requirements never covered the heartbeat during PC sleep, and no review asked. So the team fixes the process: power states join the requirements checklist and the tests, and other features are checked for the same gap. Months later, an effectiveness check finds no slow stops came back.

Pause and guess. Pause and guess: which path does each one take? A planned larger-text change is change control. One motor batch failing inspection is an NCR. The same failure in five batches is a pattern: an NCR for each, plus a CAPA.

Audit day. Then comes audit day. An auditor picks this one complaint and follows the trail, record by record, from the complaint to the CAPA's effectiveness check. Here, each record points to the next. In my experience, a break in that trail is where findings tend to start.

Season close. This season started with chaos: FDA, MDR, ISO, CAPA, SOUP. I hope it now looks more like a map, where every rule answers a question and has its place. That's how I make sense of it. Thanks for following along.
